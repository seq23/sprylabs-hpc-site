Continuity Over Intensity Rule — build an operator rhythm

Work happens in bursts followed by gaps, and the gaps get longer, so output is unpredictable and hard to plan around.

Burst working is sized to a state rather than to a schedule. Because the state is not repeatable on demand, neither is the output.

Do this first. Fix the shape of the day at three points — the open, one protected block, and the close — and hold those three constant while everything else varies.

Do not do this. Scheduling the whole day. Full scheduling is fragile and breaks on the first real interruption, and once it breaks the rest of the day is improvised.

This level fits when: the situation recurs, and it recurs often enough that the design cost amortizes. It does not fit when: this is a one-off, or you have already built three systems for it this year. Repeated building is usually avoidance of the running.

When this framework is the right one. Use it when planning a commitment you intend to keep for months, and the version you are about to commit to is the version you could do this week.

  1. Describe your worst plausible week honestly: illness, travel, a crisis at work.
  2. Size the commitment so it is still doable in that week, not in this one.
  3. Write the floor version explicitly, so that on a bad day there is something to do rather than a decision to make.
  4. Let good weeks overflow, but never let the overflow raise the floor.

Worked example: Build an operator rhythm while designing a repeatable structure

Someone sits down with this on the list. Work happens in bursts followed by gaps, and the gaps get longer, so output is unpredictable and hard to plan around.

Write the trigger first. Most systems that fail were never triggered by anything, so they ran only when the person felt like it. Fix the shape of the day at three points — the open, one protected block, and the close — and hold those three constant while everything else varies.

The framework then runs in order. First: describe your worst plausible week honestly: illness, travel, a crisis at work. Then: size the commitment so it is still doable in that week, not in this one.

What to measure. Whether the three fixed points happened, recorded daily as three binary fields. Rhythm is a frequency, so it can only be seen over weeks.

Running Continuity Over Intensity Rule for designing a repeatable structure

Effort is elastic and schedules are not. A commitment sized to a good week fails in a normal one, and each failure costs more than the extra output of the good week bought. Sizing to the worst plausible week produces a commitment that survives, and surviving is what accumulates.

The full set of failure modes for this framework, the evidence to record, and a prompt you can paste are on its framework page. This page covers the part specific to designing a repeatable structure.

What this is not. It is not an argument against hard work. Intensity is fine; it just cannot be the thing the system depends on.

The question is about design, not about today. You are deciding what will happen on a class of days, before any of them arrive.

Setup cost: an hour or two once, plus the discipline of not redesigning it every week.

Where it is strong: removes the decision from the moment. On the day, there is nothing to work out.

Where it is weak: systems designed in a good mood are sized for good days, and a system that only works on good days is worse than none.
