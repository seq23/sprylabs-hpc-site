Arbitration Engine — reduce decision fatigue (0112)

The decisions that feel hardest are small ones — what to open first, whether to reply now, which of two near-identical tasks to start. That is the signature of volume rather than difficulty.

Each of these decisions is individually cheap, so none of them registers as a cost, and the aggregate is therefore invisible while still being the thing that empties the afternoon.

Do this first. List the decisions you made yesterday that you also made the day before, then write a standing default for each one with an explicit override condition.

Do not do this. Trying to make the small decisions faster. Speed on a decision that recurs daily saves seconds; removing it saves the decision.

This level fits when: answers keep coming back plausible and unusable, which is the signature of an unbounded request. It does not fit when: you are exploring and do not yet know what good looks like. Constraints applied to exploration close it early.

When this framework is the right one. Use it when two or more things genuinely deserve the same block and the tie is not breaking on its own.

  1. Name the competing options explicitly, at most three. More than three is not arbitration, it is an unmade decision.
  2. Score each on leverage, urgency, energy fit, compounding, and downside if skipped.
  3. Say which dimension you are letting decide, before you look at the totals.
  4. Write the losing option's next review date so it is parked rather than abandoned.

Worked example: Reduce decision fatigue while converting an open-ended tool into a bounded one

Someone sits down with this on the list. The decisions that feel hardest are small ones — what to open first, whether to reply now, which of two near-identical tasks to start. That is the signature of volume rather than difficulty.

Write the form constraint first: say exactly what shape the answer must take before you describe the problem. List the decisions you made yesterday that you also made the day before, then write a standing default for each one with an explicit override condition.

The framework then runs in order. First: name the competing options explicitly, at most three. More than three is not arbitration, it is an unmade decision. Then: score each on leverage, urgency, energy fit, compounding, and downside if skipped.

What to measure. Count how many recurring decisions you converted to defaults, and how many times each override actually fired. Overrides that never fire mean the default was already right; overrides that fire constantly mean the default is wrong.

Running Arbitration Engine for converting an open-ended tool into a bounded one

Ties do not break by thinking harder, because the two options are close on the dimension you are thinking about. Scoring them on five named dimensions moves the comparison somewhere the difference is visible, and forces the trade to be stated rather than felt.

What this is not. It is not a prioritization framework for a whole backlog. It is a tie-breaker for a specific block, run in a few minutes.

The problem is not capability, it is that the tool will do anything, and anything is a bad default when you are trying to decide one thing.

Setup cost: the work of writing down constraints you have been holding implicitly.

Where it is strong: a bounded tool gives comparable answers across days, which is the precondition for noticing that something changed.

Where it is weak: constraints written too tightly produce answers that are technically compliant and useless.

Frequently asked questions

How do I know a decision is worth turning into a default?

Two tests: it recurred at least three times in the last week, and getting it wrong costs little. Decisions that recur but carry real consequence should stay decisions.
