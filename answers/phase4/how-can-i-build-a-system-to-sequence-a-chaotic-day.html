Capacity-Matched Execution Rule — sequence a chaotic day

The day contains real obligations at unpredictable times, and every plan built for it collapses before mid-morning.

The plan was written against waking hours rather than workable hours, so it was arithmetically impossible before the day started.

Do this first. List fixed constraints first, subtract them and a transition margin from the waking hours, and allocate outputs only against what remains.

Do not do this. Planning in more detail. A more detailed impossible plan is still impossible and takes longer to write.

This level fits when: the situation recurs, and it recurs often enough that the design cost amortizes. It does not fit when: this is a one-off, or you have already built three systems for it this year. Repeated building is usually avoidance of the running.

When this framework is the right one. Use it when the block in front of you does not match the state you are actually in, and the choice looks like force it or lose it.

  1. Read your current state honestly and quickly: high, medium, or low. Do not negotiate with it.
  2. Categorize your work the same way, in advance, so the matching does not require a decision when you are already low.
  3. Take work from the matching category. A low block spent on low-category work is a productive block.
  4. Note mismatches. Repeated mismatch at the same hour is a scheduling fact, not a discipline fact.

Worked example: Sequence a chaotic day while designing a repeatable structure

Someone sits down with this on the list. The day contains real obligations at unpredictable times, and every plan built for it collapses before mid-morning.

Write the trigger first. Most systems that fail were never triggered by anything, so they ran only when the person felt like it. List fixed constraints first, subtract them and a transition margin from the waking hours, and allocate outputs only against what remains.

The framework then runs in order. First: read your current state honestly and quickly: high, medium, or low. Do not negotiate with it. Then: categorize your work the same way, in advance, so the matching does not require a decision when you are already low.

What to measure. Compare planned outputs to completed outputs daily. Correct arithmetic converges those two within a week or two.

Running Capacity-Matched Execution Rule for designing a repeatable structure

Work has an energy profile and so does a block, and a mismatch produces a stalled hour that gets attributed to laziness. Keeping a categorized list means a mismatched block can be filled with work that fits rather than abandoned.

The full set of failure modes for this framework, the evidence to record, and a prompt you can paste are on its framework page. This page covers the part specific to designing a repeatable structure.

What this is not. It is not an energy-management theory or a health claim. It is a categorization of your own work plus a matching step.

The question is about design, not about today. You are deciding what will happen on a class of days, before any of them arrive.

Setup cost: an hour or two once, plus the discipline of not redesigning it every week.

Where it is strong: removes the decision from the moment. On the day, there is nothing to work out.

Where it is weak: systems designed in a good mood are sized for good days, and a system that only works on good days is worse than none.
