Decision Fatigue Reduction Protocol — reduce decision fatigue (0161)

The decisions that feel hardest are small ones — what to open first, whether to reply now, which of two near-identical tasks to start. That is the signature of volume rather than difficulty.

Each of these decisions is individually cheap, so none of them registers as a cost, and the aggregate is therefore invisible while still being the thing that empties the afternoon.

Do this first. List the decisions you made yesterday that you also made the day before, then write a standing default for each one with an explicit override condition.

Do not do this. Trying to make the small decisions faster. Speed on a decision that recurs daily saves seconds; removing it saves the decision.

This level fits when: you have a design that has survived contact with at least one real day. It does not fit when: you are still redesigning. Installing an unsettled design means reinstalling repeatedly, which is how people conclude systems do not work for them.

When this framework is the right one. Use it when the decisions that feel hardest are small ones, which is the usual sign that the count is the problem rather than the difficulty.

  1. List the decisions you made yesterday that you also made the day before. Those are the recurring ones.
  2. For each, write a default: what happens unless something specific overrides it.
  3. Write the override condition too, so the default is a rule rather than a rut.
  4. Re-decide only when the override condition fires, and review the defaults on a fixed schedule rather than continuously.

Someone sits down with this on the list. The decisions that feel hardest are small ones — what to open first, whether to reply now, which of two near-identical tasks to start. That is the signature of volume rather than difficulty.

The framework then runs in order. First: list the decisions you made yesterday that you also made the day before. Those are the recurring ones.

Then: for each, write a default: what happens unless something specific overrides it.

What to measure. Count how many recurring decisions you converted to defaults, and how many times each override actually fired. Overrides that never fire mean the default was already right; overrides that fire constantly mean the default is wrong.

Running Decision Fatigue Reduction Protocol for putting a system into daily operation

Small decisions are cheap individually and expensive in aggregate, and the aggregate is invisible because no single one felt costly. Converting recurring decisions into standing defaults removes them from the day without anyone having to decide better.
