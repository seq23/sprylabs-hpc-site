Continuity Over Intensity Rule — work when energy is low

A block arrives, the state does not match the work, and the hour goes to rereading the same paragraph.

Work has an energy profile and so does a block. Mismatch produces a stalled hour, and the stall gets attributed to discipline rather than to the mismatch.

Do this first. Categorize your work as high, medium, or low in advance, then take work from the category that matches the state you are actually in.

Do not do this. Forcing high-category work through a low block. It produces an unproductive hour and a conclusion about yourself, and neither is useful.

This level fits when: the behavior you want is stable and you want it by default. It does not fit when: you are still working out what you want. Configuring an unsettled preference makes it harder to change.

When this framework is the right one. Use it when planning a commitment you intend to keep for months, and the version you are about to commit to is the version you could do this week.

  1. Describe your worst plausible week honestly: illness, travel, a crisis at work.
  2. Size the commitment so it is still doable in that week, not in this one.
  3. Write the floor version explicitly, so that on a bad day there is something to do rather than a decision to make.
  4. Let good weeks overflow, but never let the overflow raise the floor.

Someone sits down with this on the list. A block arrives, the state does not match the work, and the hour goes to rereading the same paragraph.

Write the standing instruction, then open a completely fresh context and check it behaves the same way. Categorize your work as high, medium, or low in advance, then take work from the category that matches the state you are actually in.

The framework then runs in order. First: describe your worst plausible week honestly: illness, travel, a crisis at work. Then: size the commitment so it is still doable in that week, not in this one.

What to measure. Record the state and the category worked. Repeated mismatch at the same time of day is a scheduling fact, not a character fact.

Running Continuity Over Intensity Rule for configuring standing assistance

Effort is elastic and schedules are not. A commitment sized to a good week fails in a normal one, and each failure costs more than the extra output of the good week bought. Sizing to the worst plausible week produces a commitment that survives, and surviving is what accumulates.

The full set of failure modes for this framework, the evidence to record, and a prompt you can paste are on its framework page. This page covers the part specific to configuring standing assistance.

What this is not. It is not an argument against hard work. Intensity is fine; it just cannot be the thing the system depends on.

You are putting something in place that will be there tomorrow without being rebuilt: standing instructions, a stored template, a routine you can invoke by name.

Setup cost: a short configuration session, plus a cold test in a fresh context.

Where it is strong: it applies without you remembering to apply it, which is where most of the value is.

Where it is weak: configuration is invisible once set, so a bad rule keeps operating silently until you go and read it again.
