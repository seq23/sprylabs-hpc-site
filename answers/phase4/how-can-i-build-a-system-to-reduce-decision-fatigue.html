No Mid-Day Renegotiation Rule — reduce decision fatigue

The decisions that feel hardest are small ones — what to open first, whether to reply now, which of two near-identical tasks to start. That is the signature of volume rather than difficulty.

Each of these decisions is individually cheap, so none of them registers as a cost, and the aggregate is therefore invisible while still being the thing that empties the afternoon.

Do this first. List the decisions you made yesterday that you also made the day before, then write a standing default for each one with an explicit override condition.

Do not do this. Trying to make the small decisions faster. Speed on a decision that recurs daily saves seconds; removing it saves the decision.

This level fits when: the situation recurs, and it recurs often enough that the design cost amortizes. It does not fit when: this is a one-off, or you have already built three systems for it this year. Repeated building is usually avoidance of the running.

When this framework is the right one. Use it on any day where the plan was made in the morning and the urge to rewrite it arrives in the afternoon.

  1. Fix the plan in the morning and treat it as closed to changes from that point.
  2. When the urge to rewrite arrives, write the proposed change down instead of making it.
  3. Finish the current block anyway. The urge usually arrives at the hardest point of the block, not at a neutral moment.
  4. Review the written proposals at the end of the day and let tomorrow's plan absorb the ones that still make sense.

Worked example: Reduce decision fatigue while designing a repeatable structure

Someone sits down with this on the list. The decisions that feel hardest are small ones — what to open first, whether to reply now, which of two near-identical tasks to start. That is the signature of volume rather than difficulty.

Write the trigger first. Most systems that fail were never triggered by anything, so they ran only when the person felt like it. List the decisions you made yesterday that you also made the day before, then write a standing default for each one with an explicit override condition.

The framework then runs in order. First: fix the plan in the morning and treat it as closed to changes from that point. Then: when the urge to rewrite arrives, write the proposed change down instead of making it.

What to measure. Count how many recurring decisions you converted to defaults, and how many times each override actually fired. Overrides that never fire mean the default was already right; overrides that fire constantly mean the default is wrong.

Running No Mid-Day Renegotiation Rule for designing a repeatable structure

The morning plan and the afternoon rewrite are made by the same person in different states. The afternoon state has more fatigue and less context about why the morning chose what it chose, so its rewrites systematically favor the easier item. Freezing the plan does not make the afternoon smarter; it stops the afternoon from deciding.

What this is not. It is not rigidity for its own sake. It moves plan changes to a moment when they can be judged, which is not the middle of a hard block.

The question is about design, not about today. You are deciding what will happen on a class of days, before any of them arrive.

Setup cost: an hour or two once, plus the discipline of not redesigning it every week.

Where it is strong: removes the decision from the moment. On the day, there is nothing to work out.

Where it is weak: systems designed in a good mood are sized for good days, and a system that only works on good days is worse than none.

Frequently asked questions

How do I know a decision is worth turning into a default?

Two tests: it recurred at least three times in the last week, and getting it wrong costs little. Decisions that recur but carry real consequence should stay decisions.
