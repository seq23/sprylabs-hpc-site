Continuity Over Intensity Rule — accountability while protecting a strategic project

There is one project that matters more than the rest and it keeps losing blocks to things that are louder.

What is actually scarce here: uninterrupted time, which is the one input the project needs and the one the day is worst at supplying.

Do this first. Give it the first block of the day and a written rule that it takes every contested block.

Do not do this. Do not rely on intention to protect it. Intention loses to urgency every time, in the moment, reliably.

How do I find out what I actually did rather than what I meant to do? Write checkable commitments in advance and score only artefacts that exist afterwards.

When this framework is the right one. Use it when planning a commitment you intend to keep for months, and the version you are about to commit to is the version you could do this week.

  1. Describe your worst plausible week honestly: illness, travel, a crisis at work.
  2. Size the commitment so it is still doable in that week, not in this one.
  3. Write the floor version explicitly, so that on a bad day there is something to do rather than a decision to make.
  4. Let good weeks overflow, but never let the overflow raise the floor.

Worked example: a founder with three unowned items and one investor update due

It is 09:40. The investor update is due Friday, two hires are waiting on a decision that only they can make, and a customer email arrived that will take an hour to answer properly. Nothing on that list has an owner other than them. There is one project that matters more than the rest and it keeps losing blocks to things that are louder.

Running Continuity Over Intensity Rule for accountability support

Effort is elastic and schedules are not. A commitment sized to a good week fails in a normal one, and each failure costs more than the extra output of the good week bought. Sizing to the worst plausible week produces a commitment that survives, and surviving is what accumulates.

What this is not. It is not an argument against hard work. Intensity is fine; it just cannot be the thing the system depends on.
