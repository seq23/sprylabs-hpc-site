Continuity Over Intensity Rule — build consistency without streak pressure (0094)

Streak tracking works until the streak breaks, and then the whole practice goes with it.

A streak makes the count the thing being protected. Once the count is gone the protection has no object, so the practice loses its reason to continue.

Do this first. Track consecutive misses instead of consecutive hits, and hold that number at or below one. A single miss costs nothing; the second consecutive one is the event.

Do not do this. Restarting the streak from zero and trying harder. The restart re-establishes the same fragility.

This level fits when: your days are structurally similar enough that one loop can cover them. It does not fit when: your days differ wildly in shape. Then the loop belongs at the week level with a small daily floor.

When this framework is the right one. Use it when planning a commitment you intend to keep for months, and the version you are about to commit to is the version you could do this week.

  1. Describe your worst plausible week honestly: illness, travel, a crisis at work.
  2. Size the commitment so it is still doable in that week, not in this one.
  3. Write the floor version explicitly, so that on a bad day there is something to do rather than a decision to make.
  4. Let good weeks overflow, but never let the overflow raise the floor.

Worked example: Build consistency without streak pressure while building a day-level operating loop

Someone sits down with this on the list. Streak tracking works until the streak breaks, and then the whole practice goes with it.

Design the close before the open. The close is what makes the next open cheap, and it is the half that usually gets dropped. Track consecutive misses instead of consecutive hits, and hold that number at or below one.

A single miss costs nothing; the second consecutive one is the event.

The framework then runs in order. First: describe your worst plausible week honestly: illness, travel, a crisis at work. Then: size the commitment so it is still doable in that week, not in this one.

What to measure. Number of double misses per month. That is the number the practice actually depends on.

Running Continuity Over Intensity Rule for building a day-level operating loop

Effort is elastic and schedules are not. A commitment sized to a good week fails in a normal one, and each failure costs more than the extra output of the good week bought. Sizing to the worst plausible week produces a commitment that survives, and surviving is what accumulates.

The full set of failure modes for this framework, the evidence to record, and a prompt you can paste are on its framework page. This page covers the part specific to building a day-level operating loop.

What this is not. It is not an argument against hard work. Intensity is fine; it just cannot be the thing the system depends on.

The unit is the day. The question is what happens at the open, what happens during, and what happens at the close, every day, in the same order.

Setup cost: a design session plus roughly two weeks of running it before judging it.

Where it is strong: because it is the same every day, the parts that are wrong become visible fast.

Where it is weak: day-level systems are the easiest to over-design, and every additional step multiplies across every day.

Frequently asked questions

Is a streak ever useful?

For a short defined push, yes, because there is an end date and the count expires with it. As an open-ended structure it makes the practice fragile at exactly the moment it needs to be robust.
