Strategic Patience Filter — make a hard decision

The decision has been open for weeks. Each time you return to it you rehearse the same considerations and stop in the same place.

Rehearsal is not analysis. The options are close on the dimension you keep thinking about, so thinking about that dimension harder cannot separate them.

Do this first. Score the options on leverage, urgency, energy fit, compounding, and downside if skipped, and name which dimension you are letting decide before you total anything.

Do not do this. Gathering more information. Most of what you would gather does not change the answer, and gathering is a comfortable way to keep the decision open.

This level fits when: the situation recurs, and it recurs often enough that the design cost amortizes. It does not fit when: this is a one-off, or you have already built three systems for it this year. Repeated building is usually avoidance of the running.

When this framework is the right one. Use it when you are about to change direction and the current direction has not yet run long enough to produce evidence.

  1. Write down what evidence would tell you the current direction is wrong, in advance of looking for it.
  2. Check whether that evidence has actually arrived, or whether what arrived is discomfort.
  3. If the evidence has not arrived, name the date by which it would have, and hold until then.
  4. If it has arrived, pivot deliberately and write down what you learned so the next attempt starts further along.

Running Strategic Patience Filter for designing a repeatable structure

Most pivots happen at the point of maximum discomfort, which is usually before the point of maximum information. The filter separates the two by asking what evidence would settle the question and whether it has arrived yet.

The full set of failure modes for this framework, the evidence to record, and a prompt you can paste are on its framework page. This page covers the part specific to designing a repeatable structure.

What this is not. It is not a rule against changing direction. It is a rule about when the decision has enough information in it.

The question is about design, not about today. You are deciding what will happen on a class of days, before any of them arrive.

Setup cost: an hour or two once, plus the discipline of not redesigning it every week.

Where it is strong: removes the decision from the moment. On the day, there is nothing to work out.

Where it is weak: systems designed in a good mood are sized for good days, and a system that only works on good days is worse than none.

What if I genuinely lack information?

Apply the would-I-choose-differently test to each unknown. Usually one or two would change the answer and the rest would not, and only those one or two justify waiting.
