Arbitration Engine — reduce decision fatigue

The decisions that feel hardest are small ones — what to open first, whether to reply now, which of two near-identical tasks to start. That is the signature of volume rather than difficulty.

Each of these decisions is individually cheap, so none of them registers as a cost, and the aggregate is therefore invisible while still being the thing that empties the afternoon.

Do not do this. Trying to make the small decisions faster. Speed on a decision that recurs daily saves seconds; removing it saves the decision.

This level fits when: you want the method to still work in a year, or you use more than one assistant. It does not fit when: you have committed to one product and the convenience features would genuinely save you a step every day.

When this framework is the right one. Use it when two or more things genuinely deserve the same block and the tie is not breaking on its own.

  1. Name the competing options explicitly, at most three. More than three is not arbitration, it is an unmade decision.
  2. Score each on leverage, urgency, energy fit, compounding, and downside if skipped.
  3. Say which dimension you are letting decide, before you look at the totals.
  4. Write the losing option's next review date so it is parked rather than abandoned.

The framework then runs in order. First: name the competing options explicitly, at most three. More than three is not arbitration, it is an unmade decision. Then: score each on leverage, urgency, energy fit, compounding, and downside if skipped.

What to measure. Count how many recurring decisions you converted to defaults, and how many times each override actually fired. Overrides that never fire mean the default was already right; overrides that fire constantly mean the default is wrong.
