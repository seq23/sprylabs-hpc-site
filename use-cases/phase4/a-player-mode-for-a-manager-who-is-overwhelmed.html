Minimum Viable Day Recovery Protocol — a manager who is overwhelmed

Your output is produced by other people, and your own tasks compete with unblocking theirs.

The hardest part of the week: deciding when unblocking someone should wait.

Do this first. Say the capacity out loud before you look at the list: what fraction of a normal day is actually available.

Do not do this. Declaring a minimum day and then doing a normal one. It teaches you the minimum day was a lie, so the next time capacity drops you will not use it.

Every item on the list feels equally urgent, which is the reliable sign that none of them is being assessed. Do not re-plan. Re-planning under overwhelm produces a longer list and a stronger sense of how much there is.

When this framework is the right one. Use it on a day where the honest read is that the normal plan is not going to happen, and the choice is between a smaller day and no day.

  1. Say the capacity out loud before you look at the list: what fraction of a normal day is actually available.
  2. Pick exactly one output that would make today non-zero, and define its finished state in a sentence.
  3. Delete or defer everything else in writing, so the deferral is a decision rather than a failure.
  4. Stop when the one output is done, even if energy returns. Stopping on purpose is what makes the smaller day repeatable.

What should a manager not do while overwhelmed?

Do not re-plan. Re-planning under overwhelm produces a longer list and a stronger sense of how much there is. Stop at the stop condition. If capacity returned, bank it as an early finish, not extra scope.

What should a reader do in the first week on manager?

What should a reader do in the first week on overwhelmed?

Work one item for twenty-five minutes without opening the list. Repeat rather than re-planning. Having too much to do. Often the volume is survivable and the inability to hold one item still is not.
