The field check, on one page

Step 1: Initiation — can the first physical action be named?

If the answer is a topic rather than a movement, initiation is the stall. Rewrite the task until the first step is something a video camera could record: open the file, type the subject line, put the shoes by the door.

Step 2: Working memory — is the task being held in your head?

Anything you are remembering rather than reading is consuming capacity you need for the work itself. Externalise it. The test is whether you could walk away for an hour and resume from what is written down.

Step 3: Sequencing — is the order decided, or being decided repeatedly?

Re-deriving the order every session is a hidden tax. Write the sequence once, and let the written sequence be the authority even on days when a different order feels better.

Step 4: Time estimation — is the estimate anchored to anything?

Compare against a similar task you actually finished and how long that one took, not against how long it feels like it should take. Estimating from feeling is what turns a plan into an apology.

Step 5: Emotional regulation — is avoidance about the task or about what it represents?

Some tasks are unpleasant; some carry a verdict about you. Urgency helps with the first and worsens the second. Naming which one you are dealing with is more useful than pushing harder.

Step 6: Task switching — is there a re-entry note?

Interruptions cost most when they destroy context. A one-line note about where you stopped and what you were about to do next converts a lost session into a paused one.

Where this usually goes wrong

Reading a stall as a character verdict

"Lazy" and "undisciplined" are conclusions that predict nothing you can act on. The six functions exist because each one has a different response, and a verdict has none.

Adding structure while already overloaded

New systems feel productive and cost working memory. When the check points at working memory, the correct move is to remove items from the plan, not to add a better plan.

Applying one fix to every stall

The reason a tactic that worked last month fails this month is usually that the stall moved. Re-run the check rather than reapplying the previous answer harder.

Using the check as a substitute for assessment

The check locates friction in a task. It is not an evaluation of a person, and it does not identify or rule out any condition. Persistent difficulty that affects work, health or relationships is a reason to talk to a qualified professional, not a reason to run the checklist again.
