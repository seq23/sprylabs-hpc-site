Discipline and motivation: practical guide

Discipline and motivation is two different planning ideas: motivation describes wanting to act, while discipline commonly describes following a chosen commitment.

Quick answer

Name the action you intend to repeat and the condition that will trigger it.

What this guide is best for: readers who need to make a concrete decision about discipline and motivation.

How do you put this into practice?

Use the following sequence as a starting plan, then adjust it to the work you are responsible for.

  1. Name the action you intend to repeat and the condition that will trigger it.
  2. Use an observable cue and feasible scope instead of waiting to feel ready.
  3. Record the action you chose, its intended result and the next occasion on which you will check it. Keep the record in a place you can return to without rebuilding your setup.

How can you distinguish a difficult start from a poorly designed commitment?

Describe the starting action in terms someone could observe. Opening a draft and writing its first unresolved question is more specific than deciding to become disciplined. Then check whether the materials, permission and time needed for that action are available. If one is missing, arrange it before judging your willingness to work.

If the action is feasible but still difficult to begin, try a bounded first attempt with a defined stopping point. Record what happened without turning one attempt into a permanent description of your character. At review, distinguish resistance to a particular task from a commitment you no longer endorse. The former may need a smaller start or a clearer outcome; the latter may need an honest decision to change direction.

If another person relies on the work, communicate that decision rather than allowing the commitment to disappear through repeated postponement.
